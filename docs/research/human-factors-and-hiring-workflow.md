# Human Factors and Hiring Workflow: Research Notes

**Scope:** Evidence about attention, interruptions, work pressure, and hiring assessment that may inform Talent.OS. Facts and design implications are separated. This research does not establish that any mechanic will be fun or effective.

## Evidence

### Attention, interruptions, and workload

- Laboratory task-switching experiments found measurable time costs when switching between tasks. Preparation reduced, but did not eliminate, those costs. This supports treating a change of attention as friction, not a cost-free extra action. [Rubinstein, Meyer & Evans, “Executive Control of Cognitive Processes in Task Switching” (2001; peer-reviewed laboratory study, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance*)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.27.4.763)

- In a study of interrupted work, participants compensated for interruptions by working faster, while reporting greater stress, frustration, and time pressure. This cautions against portraying interruptions as only a loss of throughput. It does not show that every interruption has the same effect or that faster work is better work. [Mark, Gudith & Klocke, “The Cost of Interrupted Work” (2008; peer-reviewed CHI study)](https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357072)

- The World Health Organization defines burnout as a syndrome conceptualized as resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. Its dimensions include exhaustion, mental distance or cynicism, and reduced professional efficacy. WHO classifies burnout as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition. This describes chronic stress, not a single deadline or event. [WHO, “Burn-out an occupational phenomenon” (2019; official ICD-11 announcement)](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-05-2019-burn-out-an-occupational-phenomenon-international-classification-of-diseases)

### Hiring criteria, screening, and automation

- The US Office of Personnel Management describes structured interviews as assessments of job-related competencies, using past-behavior or hypothetical questions. Its guidance recommends asking candidates the same predetermined questions in the same order and evaluating responses against the same rating standards. This is one official example of standardizing evidence and assessment rather than relying only on an unstructured impression. [OPM, “Structured Interviews” (official federal hiring guidance)](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/structured-interviews/)

- The US Equal Employment Opportunity Commission describes tests and other selection procedures used to screen job applicants. It notes that a procedure that disproportionately excludes a protected group may raise discrimination concerns unless legally justified, and discusses job-relatedness, business necessity, and alternatives. This is US federal guidance, not a universal statement of law. [EEOC, “Employment Tests and Selection Procedures” (official guidance)](https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/employment-tests-and-selection-procedures)

- US Department of Justice guidance gives examples of hiring technologies used to target job advertisements, assess qualifications, conduct video interviews, administer computer-based tests, and score résumés. It warns that these tools can screen out qualified applicants with disabilities, including when a tool measures an impairment instead of the job skill it purports to measure. Employers remain responsible for applicable disability-discrimination and accommodation obligations. [US DOJ, “Algorithms, Artificial Intelligence, and Disability Discrimination in Hiring” (2022; official ADA guidance)](https://www.ada.gov/resources/ai-guidance/)

- Raghavan and colleagues analyzed claims and practices of vendors providing algorithmic pre-employment assessments, including validation, data, and prediction-target choices. The paper identifies issues to investigate; it does not show that every vendor or tool behaves alike. [Raghavan et al., “Mitigating Bias in Algorithmic Hiring: Evaluating Claims and Practices” (2020; peer-reviewed ACM FAccT paper)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3351095.3372828)

## Design implications

These are interpretations of the sources, not empirical findings about game fun:

- A plausible tension is the conflict between measured throughput and slower, job-related review. The real-world distinction between structured assessment and screening volume can make an abstract metric feel like a workplace incentive rather than an arbitrary game score.
- Interruptions may be more credible when they force a context switch and tempt the player to compensate by acting faster. The study supports the plausibility of this tension, but does not validate a specific queue, countdown, or reward.
- Automation can reduce routine volume while leaving the player accountable for the criteria it applies, what it misses, and whether those criteria reflect the job. The sources support scrutiny of job-relatedness, validation, and impact—not the claim that automation is inherently biased or that human review is always better.
- A candidate decision may feel consequential when its résumé reveals evidence that a coarse metric omits. A later appeal or follow-up is one possible fictional consequence; the sources do not establish that any particular candidate should trigger one.
- Pressure should be explored as accumulated demands, context switches, and work that remains unresolved. Treating one missed timer as burnout would conflict with WHO's chronic-work-stress framing.
- Avoid depicting rejection volume, speed, or one “fit” score as a neutral measure of good hiring. Real selection guidance emphasizes job-related criteria and monitoring for disproportionate exclusion; the DOJ also documents accessibility and accommodation concerns.

## Limits and open questions

The evidence does not determine which loop is fun, the right deadline length, how many requests players can manage, or whether support requests from upgrades will feel plausible in play. Hiring guidance cited here is US-focused. These sources can ground fictional situations and cautions; prototypes and playtests must still establish whether the resulting choices are legible, interesting, and emotionally effective.
